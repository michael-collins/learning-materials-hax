<oer-draft note="Course description from the DART 413 course plan. Revise it, then Publish."><p>Digital Fabrication Studio explores how a diverse range of technologies can translate digital designs and data into the physical world. Through hands-on experience, we will work with laser cutting, CNC routing, 3D modeling, and 3D printing in a range of materials, to realize creative projects. An understanding of the tools, workflows, materials, and interdisciplinary possibilities surrounding computer-aided design and manufacturing will emerge throughout the course. We will become skilled in the appropriate hardware and 2D/3D software through in-class exercises, one-on-one instruction, and video tutorials. Students are encouraged to consider a hybrid, analog-digital art practice that embraces both traditional craft and emerging technologies. Frequent in-progress critiques will encourage iterative processes and guide making. Assigned readings and class discussions will ask questions about the role these technologies play in the present, while prompting us to imagine their future potential.</p><h2>By the end of the course you will be able to</h2><ul><li>Distinguish 2D cutting, additive, and subtractive processes (and their formative and hybrid extensions) by what each can and cannot make, and select a process for a given form, material, and quantity with a stated rationale.</li><li>Operate the studio's laser cutter and 3D printers independently after certification, and the CNC router at the level of access the shop permits, following the shop's safety, material, and booking rules.</li><li>Prepare production-ready files — clean vectors with correct cut/score/engrave conventions, manifold meshes at correct scale and orientation, slicer and toolpath settings — and diagnose file problems before they reach a machine.</li><li>Measure and apply kerf, fit tolerance, orientation, and material constraints, and revise an object through at least three documented iterations, evaluating each against the last.</li><li>Model objects using both direct/sculptural and parametric methods, and generate a family of variants from a single definition.</li><li>Analyze digital fabrication through the histories of craft, industrial production, and the maker movement, and articulate — in writing and in critique — a position on what a machine-made object means in their own practice.</li><li>Document process and outcome — files, settings, tests, failures, finished work — in a portfolio another maker could reproduce from, and present that work publicly.</li><li>Give and receive critique that addresses concept, craft, and process, and revise work in response.</li></ul></oer-draft>
