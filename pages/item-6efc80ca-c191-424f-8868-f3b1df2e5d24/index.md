A product shot needs a studio: a floor that curves up into a back wall so there's no horizon line behind the object, and lights you place yourself. In this part you'll build that backdrop around the Airline Chair, choose the camera you'll render through, light the chair with three area lights in a three-point setup, and make two test renders in Cycles to see how the sampling level changes the noise and the render time. You'll need your textured Airline Chair from the Texturing the Airline Chair series. The video uses Blender 2.83.

Build the backdrop
------------------

1.  **Add a large floor.** <page-anchor target="#video-h2jxV2_Lu8w" value="8">0:08</page-anchor> Press Shift A and choose _Mesh_ › _Plane_, then press S and scale it up into a really large floor.
    
2.  **Extrude a back wall.** <page-anchor target="#video-h2jxV2_Lu8w" value="18">0:18</page-anchor> Press Tab for Edit Mode and 2 for edge select mode, select the back edge, and extrude it upwards, pressing Z to keep it on the Z axis. You now have a large floor and a large wall.
    
3.  **Bevel the crease.** <page-anchor target="#video-h2jxV2_Lu8w" value="33">0:33</page-anchor> Instead of a hard crease where the floor meets the wall, make a smooth curve. Press T to show the toolbar if it's hidden, choose the _Bevel_ tool and drag a bevel out of the edge. At first it makes only one bevel face, so raise _Segments_ in the _Bevel_ panel <page-anchor target="#video-h2jxV2_Lu8w" value="54">0:54</page-anchor> until the curve looks smooth.
    
    ![In Edit Mode, the floor and back wall meet in a curve of thin orange bevel faces. The Bevel tool is active in the toolbar, and the Bevel panel at the lower left shows Offset 0.358 m and Segments 9.](files/studio-backdrop-and-three-point-lighting-t0058.8.jpg)
    
    The bevel with more segments: a curve instead of a crease.
    
4.  **Shade it smooth.** <page-anchor target="#video-h2jxV2_Lu8w" value="64">1:04</page-anchor> Go back to the _Select_ tool and click anywhere, then press Tab to leave Edit Mode. The curve looks faceted, which is not how it should render, so right-click and choose _Shade Smooth_. That's your backdrop and floor.
    
5.  **Sit the chair on the floor.** <page-anchor target="#video-h2jxV2_Lu8w" value="84">1:24</page-anchor> With the chair in the scene, it's a little below the floor, so move the whole floor down. Select the plane, press G Z and move it down to the bottom of the chair <page-anchor target="#video-h2jxV2_Lu8w" value="98">1:38</page-anchor>. Wireframe mode makes the plane easier to see <page-anchor target="#video-h2jxV2_Lu8w" value="112">1:52</page-anchor>; zoom in and get it as close as you can. Don't leave the chair hovering above the floor, because sometimes you can tell. It can intersect a tiny bit, but be as precise as you can.
    
    ![The Airline Chair seen from the left in orthographic view while the floor plane is moved along the global Z axis: the plane's origin, a small orange dot, sits just below the chair's runners. The header reads D: -1.878 m along global Z.](files/studio-backdrop-and-three-point-lighting-t0108.8.jpg)
    
    Moving the floor down to the bottom of the chair, seen from the side.
    

Choose the render camera
------------------------

1.  **Add a camera if you need one.** <page-anchor target="#video-h2jxV2_Lu8w" value="144">2:24</page-anchor> Press Shift A and choose _Camera_; it appears in the Outliner. The video already has a camera set up, so it deletes the new one. If you have a lot of cameras, rename them so you know which is which.
    
2.  **Make it the scene's camera.** <page-anchor target="#video-h2jxV2_Lu8w" value="172">2:52</page-anchor> The button that looks through the camera uses the scene's camera. To choose it, open the _Scene_ properties tab (its icon looks like a cone and a sphere) and set _Camera_ at the top: clear the field, then use the eyedropper or click in the field and pick your camera.
    
    ![The Scene properties tab is open, and its Camera field is set to Camera.001. In the 3D Viewport the small chair sits on the large grey floor, with the curved back wall behind it.](files/studio-backdrop-and-three-point-lighting-t0192.8.jpg)
    
    The _Camera_ field in the _Scene_ properties decides which camera you look through and render from.
    
3.  **Select the camera.** <page-anchor target="#video-h2jxV2_Lu8w" value="211">3:31</page-anchor> Click it in the Outliner or, while you're looking through it, click the dotted outline of the camera's frame in the viewport. Its settings are in the camera properties; this part leaves them for later.
    

Light the chair
---------------

1.  **Render with Cycles.** <page-anchor target="#video-h2jxV2_Lu8w" value="238">3:58</page-anchor> Switch the viewport to rendered shading. Depending on your settings, the render engine may be _Eevee_, Blender's real-time renderer: it does a pretty good job but needs some configuration to look good. Use _Cycles_ instead (in the _Render_ properties, _Render Engine_). It takes longer to render because it isn't real time, but the renders typically look better.
    
    ![Looking through the camera in rendered shading: the chair is barely lit and the scene is dark. The viewport reads Path Tracing Sample 8/32, and the Render properties show Render Engine Cycles, Device CPU, and Sampling Render 250, Viewport 32.](files/studio-backdrop-and-three-point-lighting-t0262.0.jpg)
    
    Cycles in rendered shading, before any lights are added: the scene is dark.
    
2.  **Turn off the default light.** <page-anchor target="#video-h2jxV2_Lu8w" value="271">4:31</page-anchor> A new scene has a default point light. The video disables it (you can delete it instead) and adds its own lights.
    
3.  **Add an area light for the key light.** <page-anchor target="#video-h2jxV2_Lu8w" value="291">4:51</page-anchor> Press Shift A and choose _Light_ › _Area_: an area light gives a nice soft light. Press G Z to move it up, turn on the rotation handles and rotate it, and move it off to the front so light comes from the front <page-anchor target="#video-h2jxV2_Lu8w" value="308">5:08</page-anchor>. This is the key light, your strongest, brightest light. Press S to scale it up, which enlarges the area the light comes from <page-anchor target="#video-h2jxV2_Lu8w" value="338">5:38</page-anchor>.
    
    ![The Add menu is open over the dark rendered view, with Light highlighted and its submenu showing Point, Sun, Spot and Area; the pointer is on Area. In the Outliner the default Light is greyed out.](files/studio-backdrop-and-three-point-lighting-t0299.4.jpg)
    
    _Light_ › _Area_ in the _Add_ menu.
    
    ![The new area light, Area.001, to the right of the chair with its outline and the rotate and move handles around it. The Resize panel shows Scale 2.529 on X, Y and Z, and the floor near the chair is now faintly lit.](files/studio-backdrop-and-three-point-lighting-t0345.0.jpg)
    
    The key light, scaled up so it gives a softer light.
    
4.  **Duplicate it for the fill light.** <page-anchor target="#video-h2jxV2_Lu8w" value="343">5:43</page-anchor> A three-point setup, the kind you'll find in studio lighting for products or portraits, has lights in three key places. Press Shift D to duplicate the key light, move the copy to the other side and rotate it towards the chair. This fill light contributes less light to the scene; the key light is much brighter.
    
5.  **Set each light's power.** <page-anchor target="#video-h2jxV2_Lu8w" value="373">6:13</page-anchor> With a light selected, open its settings under the light bulb tab and change _Power_. The video starts by changing 10 W to 100 W, then sets the fill light to 80 W and the key light to 500 W. This takes trial and error: the right power depends on the scale of your scene, so you may need more or less.
    
    ![Area.001 is selected, and its light properties show the Area type, Power 500 W, Shape Square and Size 1 m. A bright pool of light falls on the floor in front of the chair; the fill light's outline is on the left.](files/studio-backdrop-and-three-point-lighting-t0407.0.jpg)
    
    The key light at 500 W, much brighter than the fill light.
    
6.  **Duplicate again for the rim light.** <page-anchor target="#video-h2jxV2_Lu8w" value="416">6:56</page-anchor> Duplicate a light, move it behind the chair and rotate it to point at the chair. This is a rim light, or a hair light: in portrait photography it highlights hair, and on an object it makes a rim of light that adds contrast between the object and its background. So the setup has a key light; a fill light, which fills the shadows with a bit of light (a reflector can do that job instead of a light); and a rim light at the back.
    
    ![A third area light, Area.003, set to 80 W, is above and behind the chair with its rotate handles showing, and the chair's back now catches the light. The Outliner lists Area.001, Area.002 and Area.003.](files/studio-backdrop-and-three-point-lighting-t0435.4.jpg)
    
    The rim light behind the chair, the third of the three lights.
    

Frame the shot
--------------

1.  **Turn the backdrop.** <page-anchor target="#video-h2jxV2_Lu8w" value="467">7:47</page-anchor> To get the backdrop behind the chair in the camera view, rotate the backdrop. You could rotate the chair instead, but here it's easier to rotate the background.
    
2.  **Lock the camera to the view.** <page-anchor target="#video-h2jxV2_Lu8w" value="482">8:02</page-anchor> To zoom and move the camera as you would the viewport, open the sidebar's _View_ tab and check _Lock Camera to View_ under _View Lock_. While it's unchecked, you can't zoom, rotate or move the render camera from the viewport.
    
    ![The sidebar's View tab is open, and the pointer is on the Lock Camera to View checkbox under View Lock. Through the camera, the chair is seen at an angle with the turned backdrop behind it; the Rotate panel shows Angle 46.7 degrees around Z.](files/studio-backdrop-and-three-point-lighting-t0495.0.jpg)
    
    _Lock Camera to View_, in the sidebar's _View_ tab.
    

Make a test render
------------------

1.  **Check the resolution.** <page-anchor target="#video-h2jxV2_Lu8w" value="523">8:43</page-anchor> _Render_ › _Render Image_ uses the scene's render settings. In the _Output_ properties (the printer icon), check the resolution. The video's file had a wide resolution set for a website banner; set something more like 1920 × 1080.
    
    ![The Output properties, Dimensions panel: Resolution X 1920 px, Y 1080 px, 100%. Through the camera, the lit chair stands on the floor with the backdrop behind it, and the area lights show as outlines.](files/studio-backdrop-and-three-point-lighting-t0558.4.jpg)
    
    A 1920 × 1080 resolution in the _Output_ properties.
    
2.  **Render.** <page-anchor target="#video-h2jxV2_Lu8w" value="562">9:22</page-anchor> Render a first time to see whether you like the composition and whether the quality is good enough: _Render_ › _Render Image_. A new window opens, and Cycles renders the image in tiles. This can take a few minutes, depending on your computer; if you have a GPU that Blender supports, you can configure Blender to use it and render faster.
    
3.  **Render again at a lower sampling level.** <page-anchor target="#video-h2jxV2_Lu8w" value="606">10:06</page-anchor> To compare, change the slot at the top of the render window to _Slot 2_ so the first render stays in _Slot 1_. Then in the _Render_ properties (the camera icon), under _Sampling_, the video's _Render_ value is 250 (yours may say 128). Lower it to 72 <page-anchor target="#video-h2jxV2_Lu8w" value="641">10:41</page-anchor>, only slightly higher than the viewport's sampling, and render again. It renders much faster.
    
4.  **Compare the two.** <page-anchor target="#video-h2jxV2_Lu8w" value="672">11:12</page-anchor> Zoom in on both renders and look at the shadows. Slot 1, at 250 samples, took over two minutes to render; slot 2, at 72, took 37 seconds. More samples take longer, but they do a better job of reducing noise, and the render is more accurate.
    
    ![The render window zoomed in on the seat cushion and a wooden side, showing Slot 2 and Time 00:37.40. The leather and wood are speckled with grain, and the Render samples field reads 72.](files/studio-backdrop-and-three-point-lighting-t0694.8.jpg)
    
    Slot 2, 72 samples: 37 seconds, and grainy.
    
    ![The same zoomed-in view in Slot 1, Time 02:21.82: the leather cushion and the wood are smooth, with much less grain than in Slot 2.](files/studio-backdrop-and-three-point-lighting-t0706.8.jpg)
    
    Slot 1, 250 samples: over two minutes, and much cleaner.
    
5.  **Balance noise against time.** <page-anchor target="#video-h2jxV2_Lu8w" value="714">11:54</page-anchor> With Cycles, you're balancing how much noise there is against how long the render takes. This is a fairly simple render and two minutes isn't long to wait, so you could raise the samples from 250 to 500 or 700 to reduce the noise further, and it would take much longer. For this render, viewed from this distance, 250 is pretty good, but 72 isn't enough: it's grainy and not an attractive render. Anything above 200 gives a decent render of this kind; look at your results to decide.
    

Wrap-up
-------

You've built a seamless backdrop, set the chair on it, chosen the render camera, and lit the chair with a key light, a fill light and a rim light. Your test renders show the trade-off at the heart of Cycles: more samples mean less noise and longer renders. In the next part you'll adjust the lighting to make the render more pleasing.
